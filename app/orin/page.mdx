## Why it exists

I built Orin for people who own a stock but have never read the company's filings. The filing is
where the real numbers are, but they're hard to get at. The XBRL tags behind them vary from company to
company, and the most useful parts of the text sit in risk factors and footnotes.

AI summaries looked like the obvious fix, and also the obvious risk. A model that rounds a revenue
figure or makes one up is worse than no summary at all. So the rule from day one was that **the AI
never produces a number.**

## What it does

- Finds the company's latest 10-Q or 10-K on EDGAR.
- Extracts seven metrics: revenue, net income, diluted EPS, operating income, gross margin, total
  assets and total liabilities, with year-over-year changes where they apply.
- Writes a plain-English summary around those figures, plus **What most people miss**: risks the
  filing mentions quietly.
- Scores **Business Health** from 1 to 10.
- Shows "not reported" for anything the company didn't report.

It explains filings. It never gives buy or sell advice.

## Architecture

Next.js App Router and TypeScript on Vercel, Tailwind and shadcn/ui for the interface, Recharts for the
revenue trend, the Claude API for prose, SEC EDGAR's XBRL APIs for data, Supabase (Postgres with
row-level security) for storage and Clerk for sign-in.

## Key decisions

### Numbers in, prose out

Before the model sees anything, the extracted figures are formatted into exact strings: "$109.42
billion", "+16.4%". The prompt says those strings are the only numbers it may use, copied as written.
After it responds, a check pulls every number out of the summary and compares it with that allowed set.
If even one doesn't match, Orin withholds the whole summary and shows the extracted figures on their
own. A summary that fails the check is never cached.

### Business Health without an LLM

The score is arithmetic. Six signals (revenue growth, revenue trend, margin direction, profitability,
operating income trajectory and leverage) are each normalized, weighted and mapped to 1-10. The same
filing always gets the same score, and every factor shown comes from an extracted figure. If too
few signals can be computed, Orin says there isn't enough data instead of showing a misleading score.

### Cache by accession number

Every SEC filing has a unique accession number. Orin's first step is a cheap EDGAR lookup that returns
the latest filing's number, and the cache is keyed on it. When a company files something new, the
number changes and the cache misses on its own. There's no expiry time to tune. A hit skips the
company facts, the filing text and the AI call entirely.

### Tag fallbacks, and a calculated fourth quarter

Companies label the same figure differently, so each metric has an ordered list of XBRL tags to try.
Banks report revenue under a different tag than retailers, and REITs report net income under another.
Companies also file no 10-Q for their fourth fiscal quarter, so the revenue chart computes that
quarter as the annual total minus the three reported quarters and labels it "calculated."

## Testing

I correctness-tested Orin on five filer types that report in different shapes: Apple, JPMorgan,
Walmart, Rivian and Realty Income. A bank and a REIT break assumptions that hold for Apple and Walmart,
which is the point. A verification script runs all five end to end: it extracts the figures, downloads
the actual filing from sec.gov, and confirms every extracted value appears in the document as
presented. That catches a wrong tag or a wrong period.

## Hardening

An uncached lookup costs real money: a large filing excerpt goes to the Claude API. So the expensive
path is guarded.

- **Server-side validation.** Tickers are checked against a strict pattern before any outside call.
- **Per-IP rate limiting.** The limit applies only to cache misses, so normal browsing never hits it.
- **Request de-duplication.** Concurrent requests for the same uncached filing share one in-flight
  analysis, so two visitors can't trigger two AI calls for one filing.
- **One EDGAR client.** Every request carries the User-Agent the SEC requires and is spaced to stay
  under EDGAR's limit of 10 requests per second.
- **Row-level security.** Users can only read their own saved stocks. The shared cache has row-level
  security with no policies, so only the server can touch it.
- **Graceful failure.** A cache error becomes a miss, and a failed summary still shows the numbers.
